# The Generalist's Field Manual

A condensed, high-leverage summary of *[A Reading List for Generalists](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/sH4cFDDjRdGrn3p2o/a-reading-list-for-generalists)* by Dylan Bowman.

**The premise:** Generalists improve fastest not by reading more, but by *rigorously applying* a small set of ideas to work they actually care about. This document distills each reading to its operating instructions — the lessons you can act on tomorrow.

Use it like a checklist, not an essay. Each entry: the one idea that matters, then the moves.

---

## I. DISPOSITIONAL — *Who you have to become*

### 1. What We Look for in Founders — Paul Graham
**Core:** A handful of traits predict who builds things that work.
- **Determination > intelligence.** The deciding trait is refusing to be deterred, not raw brainpower.
- **Flexibility** — hold the destination firmly, the route loosely. Be willing to throw out the plan, not the goal.
- **Imagination** — the best ideas look like bad ideas at first; that's *why* they're available.
- **Naughtiness** — a habit of bending rules others treat as load-bearing.
- **Friendship** — the ability to work with people through strain is itself a survival trait.

### 2. Relentlessly Resourceful — Paul Graham
**Core:** The opposite of "hapless." The single quality that best predicts whether someone makes things happen.
- When blocked, **find a way around** rather than waiting for the block to clear or for permission.
- Be the person who, dropped into a problem with no map, *generates options* instead of reporting that there are none.
- This is the trait to actively train in yourself and screen for in collaborators.

### 3. Shut Up and Do the Impossible! — Eliezer Yudkowsky
**Core:** "Impossible" usually means "I haven't tried with everything I have."
- Distinguish **"impossible" (genuinely ruled out by physics)** from **"impossible" (I gave up and labeled it)**. Most are the latter.
- Attack hard problems as if your life depended on the answer — not as if you're "giving it a shot."
- Don't pre-concede. Effort spent *proving* a thing can't be done is often effort that would have done it.

### 4. Be Impatient — Ben Kuhn
**Core:** Impatience isn't emotion; it's the reflex of asking *"how could this take less time?"* The real payoff of speed is that **you end up doing different things** — faster feedback changes what's possible.
- **Reply fast.** Move people through hiring/sales/decisions quickly to keep momentum.
- **Kill friction** in anything you do often — typing speed, shortcuts, deploys. Make common actions feel instant.
- **Bias to action** on reversible decisions; do small tasks now instead of batching.
- Stay inside the **OODA loop** — observe-orient-decide-act faster than the problem (or competitor) moves.
- *Caveat:* speed is for reversible bets with clear feedback. Slow down for irreversible, high-stakes calls.

### 5. How to Be More Agentic — Cate Hall
**Core:** Agency is a *learnable* skill — "manifest determination to make things happen" — found at the edges: **things you'll do that others won't** because they're uncomfortable.
- **Court rejection on purpose.** Ask for unreasonable things to recalibrate what's actually possible. "No" is data, not damage.
- **Engineer real feedback** — make it anonymous (a form, not a face) to strip out social friction.
- **Network randomly.** Best collaborations come from low-intent meetings, not strategically "relevant" ones.
- **Treat every trait as trainable** — confidence, charisma, agency itself.
- **Accept the "moat of low status."** Tolerating being bad at new things is the filter most people fail.
- **Protect energy** — falling agency is an early burnout signal. Hard boundaries (offline by 6, sabbaths).

## II. STRATEGY — *Deciding well with incomplete information*

### 6. Humans Are Not Automatically Strategic — Anna Salamon
**Core:** *The most-recommended item on the list.* Intelligence does **not** automatically produce strategic behavior. Most smart people leave enormous value on the table by skipping cheap, obvious strategic moves.
- **Actually aim.** Ask what you're *really* trying to achieve, then check whether today's activity serves it. Most don't.
- **Spend money/time to buy information and capability** — courses, experiments, hiring help — instead of defaulting to grinding harder.
- **Try plans on paper before in reality**; reason through multiple options rather than seizing the first.
- **Track results and update.** Notice when something isn't working and change it.
- **Identify your own blindspots** — the specific ways *you* are predictably unstrategic. Bowman credits this single shift with major career value.

### 7. Learning By Writing — Holden Karnofsky
**Core:** Organize learning around **writing, not reading.** Writing forces you to find *which version of a claim you actually care about* and points research at what matters.
- **Pick a claim** that's "important if true, and might be true."
- **Read 1–3 pieces per side**, then **write a premature, bold hypothesis** before you feel qualified.
- **Attack your own draft** — play devil's advocate, list the gaps.
- **Research only the subquestions that could change your mind.** Skip around documents; don't read linearly.
- **Revise hard** — flip sides if warranted. Loop hypothesis → critique → research → revise until stable.
- **Then** share for outside feedback.

### 8. How I Select Alignment Research Projects — Ethan Perez (w/ Balesni & Sleight)
**Core:** A reusable filter for choosing *what to work on*, beyond its AI-safety framing.
- Prefer projects that are **high-impact if they work** *and* **fast to get signal on** — cheap experiments before expensive ones.
- **Generate many candidate ideas; kill most quickly.** Quantity upstream, ruthlessness downstream.
- Pick work that **builds compounding skill and surface area**, not just a one-off result.

### 9. Do Things That Don't Scale — Paul Graham
**Core:** Early on, do the manual, unscalable, embarrassing work — because **it gives you maximal information**.
- **Recruit users by hand,** one at a time. Don't wait for them to come.
- **Deliver an overwhelmingly good experience** to a tiny number of people; learn from the friction.
- Unscalable work is **a sensor, not just labor** — it's how you find out what's actually true about your project.

### 10. Impact, Agency, and Taste — Ben Kuhn
**Core:** On strong teams the bottleneck isn't ability — it's finding **high-leverage work** (max impact-per-hour). That needs *agency* (execution) and *taste* (intuition for what works).
- **Agency:** work backward from the root goal (find shorter paths than you were handed); **don't ask permission** — treat bold work as a portfolio of bets; **make success inevitable** by owning outcomes, tracking progress, surfacing problems early.
- **Taste:** find where *"everyone else mysteriously sucks"* — that's your edge; **predict outcomes explicitly** before deciding; **reflect relentlessly** (weekly reviews compound).

### 11. Comment on "Against Almost Every Theory of Impact of Interpretability" — Richard Ngo
**Core:** A transferable reasoning move: **winning whack-a-mole doesn't disprove the strategy.**
- Refuting today's specific instances of an approach does **not** discredit the general forward-chaining method behind it.
- Beware critiques that defeat current examples and declare the whole direction dead. Separate *"this instance failed"* from *"this class of approach is wrong."*

## III. PROJECT LEADERSHIP — *Getting it done with boots on the ground*

### 12. How I've Run Major Projects — Ben Kuhn
**Core:** Project management is a rare, high-leverage skill. Big projects are **incomplete-information problems**; gathering the missing information *is* the work, and the top failure mode is **not freaking out soon enough.**
- **Focus intensely** — clear the schedule; keep it your "top idea." Just processing updates can eat 6+ hours/day in a crisis.
- **Keep a detailed "plan for victory"** — concrete steps ending in the goal. It exposes gaps early and tells you when to escalate, cut scope, or ask for help.
- **Run fast OODA loops** — maintain a living list of biggest open questions; resolve them in parallel.
- **Over-communicate** — repeat goals far more than feels necessary; shift async → sync when coordination gets critical.
- **Delegate management, not just tasks** — past ~10 people, hand subprojects crisp high-level goals; pick organizers over deep experts as leads.
- **Toolkit:** master "landing page" doc (goal, staffing, roadmap, open questions), ranked priority lists, shared channels (not DMs), short weekly broadcasts, retrospectives.

### 13. Categories of Leadership on Technical Teams — Ben Kuhn
**Core:** "Leadership" is several distinct jobs; teams fail when one is silently unowned.
- Name the functions — e.g. **direction-setting, project management, technical leadership, people management** — and check each has an owner.
- One person can wear several hats, but **no hat can be vacant.** Diagnose gaps by which category nobody is holding.

### 14–15. #define CTO / #define CTO @ OpenAI — Greg Brockman
**Core:** How a person with a **spiky skillset finds their place** in an organization — by shaping the role around their strengths instead of fitting a template.
- A title (like CTO) has **no fixed definition** — define it by what the organization actually needs and what you uniquely provide.
- **Go where the leverage is**, not where the org chart points. Continuously redefine your role as the company's needs change.
- Spiky people add the most by leaning into the spike, not sanding it down to be well-rounded.

### 16. Tips for Empirical Alignment Research — Ethan Perez
**Core:** Domain-specific in framing, but really a guide to being **an excellent (managed) individual contributor in any field.**
- **Make your work legible** — others should be able to see what you did, why, and whether it worked, without excavating.
- **Scope timelines realistically** and communicate them; manage *up* as much as you execute.
- Optimize for **fast, informative iterations** over polished, slow ones.

## IV. INTERPERSONAL / ORGANIZATIONAL — *Reading systems and people*

### 17. The Elephant in the Brain — Kevin Simler & Robin Hanson *(book)*
**Core:** *The best general human-psychology book* — much of behavior is driven by **hidden motives** (status, signaling) that we conceal **even from ourselves.** Read at minimum the opening section on signaling theory.
- **Stated reasons ≠ real reasons.** Decode others by their *incentives and signals*, not their explanations.
- **The lens points at you too.** Your own noble-sounding motives are partly status-seeking — and that's normal, not shameful.
- Designing institutions/products that **let people signal honestly** beats fighting human nature.

### 18. Accountability Sinks — Martin Sustrik
**Core:** An **accountability sink** is what forms when human judgment is replaced by formal process, so that **nobody decides and nobody is responsible.**
- When a system fails you, the sink means **there's no one to appeal to** — the process, not a person, "decided."
- Sinks **paper over problems instead of solving them** and breed perverse incentives (you only get an exception by escalating to aggression).
- **Process isn't the enemy** — it brings efficiency, safety, retained knowledge. *Strategic* accountability limits (blameless postmortems, protecting risk-takers) are good.
- **Design test:** self-imposed guidelines preserve judgment; top-down mandates incentivize blind compliance. Keep a human accountable for outcomes that need judgment.

### 19. Keep Your Identity Small — Paul Graham
**Core:** You reason badly about anything you've made part of your **identity.** The fewer labels you adopt, the clearer you think.
- A topic becomes unthinkable once "people like me believe X" — debate turns to defense.
- **Don't recruit beliefs into your sense of self.** Hold positions as current best guesses, not as who you are.
- Keep identity small so you can **change your mind cheaply.**

### 20. How to Disagree — Paul Graham
**Core:** A hierarchy of disagreement, from name-calling up to **refuting the central point.** Aim for the top; demand it of yourself.
- The ladder (low → high): name-calling → ad hominem → tone → contradiction → counterargument → refutation → **refuting the central point.**
- **Steelman first:** restate their strongest version, then refute *that*. Attacking tone or the person is a tell that you have no real counter.

## V. BONUS — *From the comments*

### 21. The Part-Time Creator Manifesto — Swyx
**Core:** You can build a meaningful creative/public body of work **without quitting your job** — compounding part-time output beats waiting for the "someday" leap.

### 22. Becoming a Full-Time Creator as a Software Engineer — Pragmatic Engineer
**Core:** A sober, contrarian look at the economics and trade-offs of going independent — **derisk before you leap**, and know the real numbers.

### 23. The Ground State Is Motion — Richard Ngo
**Core:** Default to **moving.** Treat action as the resting state and stillness as the thing requiring justification — momentum, not deliberation, is the baseline.

---

## The Whole List in One Breath

> **Become** relentlessly resourceful and determined; treat agency, taste, and even charisma as **trainable**. **Be impatient** — speed changes what's possible. Don't assume intelligence makes you strategic; **actually aim**, buy information, and learn by **writing, not reading**. Do the **unscalable** things that teach you the most, and pick work that's **high-impact and fast to test**. Run projects with a **plan for victory**, fast OODA loops, and relentless over-communication — and **escalate early**. See people's **hidden motives** (including your own), keep your **identity small**, refute the **central point**, and never let a **process become the place accountability goes to die.**
