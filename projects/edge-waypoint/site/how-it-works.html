The whole deal, in plain terms

Exactly what you're buying, what it costs, and what you walk away with.

Most consulting websites are vague about this part. This page is the opposite. If you read it and think "that's not for us," you've saved us both a meeting.

  1. The visit · free · 90 minutes · at your place

    We walk through a normal week.

    We sit down with you and two or three of your key people. Not "what are your pain points" — I'll ask things like "walk me through what happens when a job comes in on a Friday afternoon." People mention things they've stopped noticing. That's where the money usually is.

    Within two days you get a short write-up: the problems we found, roughly what each one costs you a year, and what I'd do about each. Some of those answers will be "buy this cheap tool" or "change this handoff" — free advice, yours to act on. The write-up is yours either way.

  2. The map · $[X] fixed · 2 weeks

    I get into the weeds.

    If a few problems look worth a closer look, I spend about two weeks shadowing the people who do the work and digging through the systems you're paying for. Most businesses use about a fifth of what their software can do. I also check whether your records are in good enough shape to build on — because if they're not, that's the real first job and I'll tell you so.

    You get a ranked list: what each problem costs, what fixing it would take, what I'd build or buy or change, and the order I'd do it in. Including, in plain terms, the ones I think you should leave alone.

  3. The fix · $[X] fixed · 4 weeks, hard stop

    We fix one thing and measure it.

    We pick one problem — usually the one with the best ratio of money-saved to fuss. Before I build anything, we write down how the work goes today: how long it takes, how often it goes wrong. That's the bar the fix has to clear.

    Someone on your team owns the new way of working from week two. Not me — I go home and you don't. By week four, one of two things is true. Either the numbers beat the old way and you keep it, or they don't and I tell you to stop. You get that recommendation in writing either way.

  4. Afterwards · $[X]/month · optional, cancel anytime

    I keep an eye on it.

    Things that run on software drift. Systems update, volumes change, something that worked in March hiccups in August. For a monthly fee I watch what we built, fix the small stuff before you notice it, and send you a one-page note each month with the numbers. Most clients keep this; nobody's locked into it.

The part where I tell you to stop paying me

It's in every fix proposal, word for word: if the new system doesn't beat your old numbers by the end of week four, I'll recommend killing it — in writing — and tell you what I'd do instead.

You'll never be asked to fund "just a few more weeks of tweaking." You're paying me for the honest answer, not for endless tinkering.

Questions I get a lot

Is this an AI thing?

Only when AI is honestly the right tool, which is less often than the ads suggest. Most of what I fix is duller than that — information getting retyped, things waiting on other things. When AI genuinely earns its keep (usually reading messy stuff like emails and PDFs), I use it, with a person checking its work.

Do I need to be technical?

No. If you can describe what annoys you, that's the whole job requirement.

Who owns what you build?

You do. It runs in your accounts, under your logins, documented in plain English. If I get hit by a bus, your stuff keeps working.

What if we're too small for this?

If the math doesn't work, I'll tell you on the first call. It costs you twenty minutes to find out.

What does the workshop cost?

Nothing. Ninety minutes of your team's time, and you keep the write-up whether we ever work together or not.

Got twenty minutes?

Tell me what's eating your week. Worst case, you get a free opinion.
